# Learning notes

This lab teaches retrieve-then-read RAG by running. Every number below is generated by the
committed eval, never typed by hand. A CI check (`make check-learning-numbers`) fails if a
tagged figure drifts from the results file.

Two kinds of statement appear here. "Observed in this lab" states a number this lab
produced. A "[VERIFY]" claim is general and is not proven by this lab's data.

The canonical run uses the `bm25` retriever, `chunk_size` 32 tokens, `chunk_overlap` 8
tokens, and `top_k` 5. It turns 20 documents into 87 chunks <!--lab:eval_results.json:chunk_count-->.
It scores 14 <!--lab:eval_results.json:metrics.n--> answerable questions.

## Chunk

A chunk is a fixed-size window of a document, measured in whitespace tokens. The retriever
ranks chunks, not whole documents. A smaller chunk is more precise but can split a fact in
two.

Analogy: a chunk is one index card cut from a long manual. A short card holds one fact. A
very short card can cut a sentence in half, so the fact lands on no single card.

Observed in this lab: the canonical run cut 20 documents into 87 chunks
<!--lab:eval_results.json:chunk_count-->. Shrinking `chunk_size` from 32 to 16 tokens
dropped recall@5 from 0.9643 <!--lab:results/run_chunk32.json:metrics.recall@5--> to 0.7143
<!--lab:results/run_chunk16.json:metrics.recall@5-->, because small chunks split facts.

## Top-k

`top_k` is how many chunks the retriever returns per question. A larger `top_k` finds more
relevant chunks but also feeds the reader more noise.

Analogy: top_k is how many index cards you pull before you stop looking. Pull too few and
you miss the card with the answer. Pull too many and you carry cards you do not need.

Observed in this lab: at `top_k` 5 the canonical recall@5 is 0.9643
<!--lab:eval_results.json:metrics.recall@5-->. At `top_k` 1 recall drops to 0.4286
<!--lab:eval_results.json:saturation.recall@1-->, so the first card is often not enough.

## recall@k

recall@k is the share of a question's relevant chunks that appear in the top k. It answers
"of the chunks that could answer this, how many did we retrieve?".

Analogy: recall is how many of the right index cards made it into your hand. If three cards
hold the answer and you grabbed two, recall is two thirds.

Observed in this lab: canonical recall@5 is 0.9643
<!--lab:eval_results.json:metrics.recall@5-->. It is below 1.0 because one question
(`q07`) has a second relevant chunk that the cutoff excludes (see the worked example).

## hit@k

hit@k is 1.0 when at least one relevant chunk is in the top k, and 0.0 otherwise. It is a
looser bar than recall: it asks only whether the reader got something useful.

Analogy: hit@k is whether you grabbed at least one correct card, even if you missed others.
One correct card is often enough for the reader to answer.

Observed in this lab: canonical hit@5 is 1.0 <!--lab:eval_results.json:metrics.hit@5-->.
Every answerable question put at least one relevant chunk in the top 5.

## MRR (mean reciprocal rank)

MRR rewards putting a relevant chunk high. The score for one question is 1 divided by the
rank of its first relevant chunk. The run reports the mean across questions.

Analogy: MRR is how near the top of the pile the first correct card sits. A correct card on
top scores 1. A correct card fifth from the top scores one fifth.

Observed in this lab: canonical MRR is 0.6726 <!--lab:eval_results.json:metrics.mrr-->. The
first relevant chunk sits near the top for most questions, not always first.

## nDCG@k

nDCG@k also rewards rank, with a gentler, logarithmic discount, and it normalises against
the best possible ordering. A score of 1.0 means the relevant chunks sit in the ideal
positions.

Analogy: nDCG is a graded tidiness score for the pile. A tidy pile with correct cards on
top scores near 1. A messy pile with correct cards buried scores lower.

Observed in this lab: canonical nDCG@5 is 0.7423 <!--lab:eval_results.json:metrics.ndcg@5-->.

## Baselines

A retrieval number means nothing without a floor a dumb retriever cannot clear. The random
baseline ranks chunks at random. The lead baseline returns only the first chunk of each
document. Both must score well below BM25.

Analogy: the baselines are a coin flip and a habit of always reading page one. If your real
method cannot beat those, it is not a method.

Observed in this lab: BM25 beats the baselines by a margin of 0.8929
<!--lab:eval_results.json:baselines.margin-->. The random baseline scores 0.0714
<!--lab:eval_results.json:baselines.random_recall--> and the lead baseline scores 0.0
<!--lab:eval_results.json:baselines.lead_recall-->.

## Saturation guard

An easy corpus hides regressions. If recall is 1.0 at the configured k and also at k equal
to 1, the gate fails on purpose. A test that cannot drop cannot warn you.

Analogy: a quiz where every answer is the first option tests nothing. If the top card is
always right, the ranking is not being measured.

Observed in this lab: recall@1 is 0.4286 <!--lab:eval_results.json:saturation.recall@1-->,
well below 1.0, so the corpus can still detect a regression.

## citation_resolvable

The stub reader's generation check is named `citation_resolvable`, not `faithfulness`. It
asks only whether every cited chunk id exists in the retrieved set. A resolvable citation
proves the id is real, not that the chunk supports the claim.

Analogy: citation_resolvable checks that each footnote points at a real page in the book.
It does not read the page to confirm the page says what the footnote claims.

Observed in this lab: canonical citation_resolvable is 1.0
<!--lab:eval_results.json:gen_checks.citation_resolvable-->. The reader only cites chunks it
retrieved.

## lexical_support

lexical_support scores the n-gram overlap between the answer and the cited chunk text. It is
a weak, deterministic proxy for support: high overlap means the chunk literally contains the
answer's phrasing. A citation to an unsupporting chunk scores near zero (the negative
control in the tests).

Analogy: lexical_support checks that the footnote's page actually contains the sentence you
quoted. A footnote pointing at an unrelated page fails this check.

Observed in this lab: canonical lexical_support is 1.0
<!--lab:eval_results.json:gen_checks.lexical_support-->, because the stub reader copies its
answer from the chunk it cites. A real model would score lower and more honestly.

## cite-or-refuse

The reader refuses when no retrieved chunk lexically supports the question. A refusal
returns no citation. The run reports `refusal_correct` (refused an unanswerable question),
`false_refusal` (refused an answerable one), and `wrong_refusal` (answered an unanswerable
one).

Analogy: cite-or-refuse is a librarian who says "I cannot find that in these books" instead
of inventing a page number. Silence beats a confident wrong answer.

Observed in this lab: the reader refused all 4 unanswerable questions
(`refusal_correct` is 4 <!--lab:eval_results.json:refusals.refusal_correct-->). It made 0
wrong refusals <!--lab:eval_results.json:refusals.wrong_refusal--> and 0 false refusals
<!--lab:eval_results.json:refusals.false_refusal--> on the canonical run. Critical-question
recall is 1.0 <!--lab:eval_results.json:critical.recall-->.

Honesty note: the stub reader is a FORMAT path, not a real model. It cannot read for
meaning. The near-miss unanswerable questions carry a word that is absent from the corpus,
so the lexical support drops below the refusal threshold and the reader refuses. A real
model is needed to refuse a near-miss that shares every word with the corpus. [VERIFY] A
learned reader with constrained decoding usually refuses unsupported questions better than a
lexical rule.

## Worked example: a relevant chunk excluded by the cutoff

Observed in this lab: question `q07` asks for the minimum multipart upload part size. Its
answer span appears in two overlapping chunks. One chunk (`nimbus-api-files#2`) ranks third
and is retrieved. The second chunk (`nimbus-api-files#3`) ranks 66th. With `top_k` 5 that
second chunk is EXCLUDED. This is why canonical recall@5 is 0.9643, not 1.0. This is an
observation of the cutoff cost, not a claim about why the second chunk ranks low.

## 10 interview questions

1. Define recall@k, hit@k, MRR, and nDCG@k. When does hit@k look good while recall@k is
   poor, and which would you gate on for a RAG system?
2. Relevance here is by span containment, recomputed per chunk set. Why is that more robust
   than labelling relevance by a fixed chunk id?
3. A fact is split across a chunk boundary. What happens to recall, and how do `chunk_size`
   and `chunk_overlap` change the outcome?
4. Why must a retrieval gate include weak baselines, and what does it mean if a baseline
   nearly matches your retriever?
5. What is the saturation guard, and why can a recall of 1.0 be a failing result rather than
   a passing one?
6. The stub metric is called `citation_resolvable`, not `faithfulness`. Explain the
   difference and why the naming matters.
7. Define a false refusal and a wrong refusal. Which is worse for a support assistant, and
   why does the answer depend on the use case?
8. Why does a lexical cite-or-refuse rule fail on a near-miss question that shares the
   corpus vocabulary? What would catch it?
9. Your recall@5 is 0.96 and your reader still answers wrong. List three failure points
   between retrieval and the final answer.
10. You want a gate that catches regressions without tripping on noise. How do you set a
    tolerance band, and how do you prove the gate can actually fail?
